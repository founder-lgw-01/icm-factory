## Part 4: The 9 stages, 1 at a time

First, the fork. Before anything gets built, the line asks 1 question. Is
this a YouTube video, or a VSL, or both? A YouTube video is content. It earns
a subscriber and ends in a package and a blog post. A VSL is a closer. It
sells or books a call, and it ends on a page built around it. Both means 1
body recorded once with 2 endings. The answer gets written down once and
never asked again.

**Stage 1: The gameplan.** Your keyword, your hook and your title get locked
at the top, before anything else exists. You never make a video and then go
looking for traffic. The file is `01-GAMEPLAN.md`, and it carries the lock
block that every later stage reads.

**Stage 2: The script.** Numbered slides, never prose. 1 complete spoken
thought per slide, 14 words or fewer, because you read at about 2.5 words a
second and a slide holds under 6 seconds. Every number in the file is counted
by a script. You approve it before anything moves.

**Stage 3: The spec.** The stage everyone skips, and the most expensive skip
on the line. It names the render engine on line 1, then every shot, every
color rule, and what must never appear on screen. The 2 videos that ever
skipped it both had to be re-recorded.

**Stage 4: The reader.** Your script becomes a full-screen slideshow you
record from. 1 slide at a time, your colors, your font. A gate script
checks the actual bytes before you ever see it. Wrong colors, long slides, or
stray punctuation and it fails before it wastes your take.

**Stage 5: The recording, then the storyboard.** You record. While you
record, NOTHING else runs on your machine. Then the storyboard gets built
from your actual audio, 1 row per spoken sentence, and you approve it on 1
page before anything renders.

**Stage 6: The 90 second render.** The first 90 seconds as a real playable
file, rendered in the cloud. You watch it. Nothing full-length renders before
you approve this file.

**Stage 7: The full render.** Measured assembly, loudness checked on the
output, and a cut-point audit of the finished file. Your voice is never cut.
Not once, not by a frame.

**Stage 8: The package.** The video becomes a community drop: the post, 2 to
5 files that each end in a prompt your members can run, a term sheet, and the
zip. Plus the publish kit: your locked title, the description in your voice,
real chapter timestamps, 2 thumbnails.

**Stage 9: The blog post.** Every live video gets a post behind it on your
site. It claims its own keyword, embeds the video, and links back into your
older posts. On the VSL fork, stages 8 and 9 become the page the video sits
on instead.

**And the machine question, because it cost me real days.**

I started this line on a laptop with 8GB of memory. A local render roped all
of it. The fans screamed, the mouse froze, and the machine was unusable for
most of an hour per render. 1 night, 2 local renders back to back froze the
machine and burned 90 minutes of a same-day upload window.

| Your machine | Rendering locally | Rendering in the cloud |
|---|---|---|
| 8GB laptop | Freezes. Unusable while it runs | Fine |
| 16GB or more | Runs, but the machine is hostage until it finishes | Fine |
| Any machine | You stop working every render | You keep working |

That last row is the real spec sheet. The measure is not render speed. It is
whether your machine stays yours. In the cloud, that 8 video batch cost about
45 cents and I kept working the entire time.

Where the renders go: AWS. You may hear that called cloud rendering. It means
Amazon's computers build the video and hand the file back. A new account
starts with $100 in free credits, and AWS added $20 more to mine once I
started using it.

## Part 6: The hot stoves

I touched all 6. The trap and the lesson live here. The repairs live in the
kit, wired into the stages so they run without you remembering them.

**Stove 1: The skipped spec.** A live screen share looked too simple to spec,
so the spec got skipped. The reader came back with a 27 word slide, and it
reached the microphone. 47 minutes got recorded off it before anyone caught
it.
**The lesson:** the simple video needs the spec MORE. Skipping a stage is
never faster. The kit's gap checker now refuses to walk past a missing stage.

**Stove 2: The starved recording.** A 17 minute take was recorded while
background jobs copied files and pushed a big upload on the same machine. The
capture starved. 6 seconds of black under the voice, and a 5 second drift
between screen and mic. Nobody found it until after 2 renders.
**The lesson:** while you record, nothing runs. Not an upload, not a copy,
nothing. The line stops every job the moment you say you're recording.

**Stove 3: The spliced voice.** An edit inserted holds on top of pauses the
take already had, and 4 of the cuts landed inside words. Every one was
audible, in the hook, the most expensive 15 seconds of the video.
**The lesson:** the voice is never cut. A pause the edit needs is a pause you
already performed. The line proves it on the waveform before assembly.

**Stove 4: The frozen laptop.** The 8GB story from Part 5. The render worked.
The business stopped. Moving renders to AWS was the single biggest unlock on
this line. The machine came back, the credits covered every render, and after
3 weeks my card still reads $0.
**The lesson:** renders leave the machine. AWS with its free credits is the
move. The clock is not the measure. Your free machine is.

**Stove 5: The stale script.** A reader got built from the version of the
script in the AI's memory instead of the saved file, and it shipped missing a
beat the owner had added. It cost 20 minutes of an upload window.
**The lesson:** the file on disk is the only truth. Every stage re-reads it
from disk, even 2 minutes after writing it.

**Stove 6: The invented number.** A page passed every automated check and
still held 8 made-up figures. Days that meant nothing. Durations nobody
measured. Even a fake receipt about my own files.
**The lesson:** a number is counted, measured, or it does not ship. The line
counts every script with a script and marks the unmeasured as unmeasured.
