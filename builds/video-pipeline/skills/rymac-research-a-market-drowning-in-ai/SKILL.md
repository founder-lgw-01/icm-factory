# Research a market drowning in AI

Find out what is actually happening in the market for simple, folder-based AI
systems. The output is a verdict and an offer, never a summary.

Read these before writing anything. They are the fixed part:

- `references/the-4-signs.md` for the diagnostic, the ladder that sets the
  price, the cost model, and the worked example
- `references/icm-market-map.md` for what ICM is, where the hole is, and the
  35-build corpus that is the author's evidence
- `references/delivery-economics.md` for build hours, the retainer trap, and
  the 65% floor

## Step 1: frame it

Pull these out of the prompt before any research runs:

- **The segment.** Who, specifically. "Small businesses" is not a segment.
- **The pain hypothesis.** What the owner suspects hurts.
- **The offer hypothesis.** What they are thinking of selling.
- **The price hypothesis.** If none is given, ASK for one before running.

If only an observation was given, write the hypotheses and state them up front
so the research can prove or break them.

**Then name the starting bet in one line**, so the research is a test and not a
search for agreement. The default bet this market was built on: the enterprise
AI wave backfired, that buzz rolled downhill, small and medium businesses put
the AI hat on somebody who did not want it, and now they are paying for a mess
they cannot leave.

## Step 2: fan out, 6 researchers, always all 6

Launch all 6 at once. Each uses web search and returns findings with sources,
never opinions.

1. **The pain map.** Where they complain, in their own words: the small
   business and AI subreddits, owner groups, LinkedIn posts and comments,
   reviews of AI tools, comments under AI-for-business videos. Return VERBATIM
   quotes with links. The exact phrasing becomes the copy later, so paraphrase
   is worthless here.
2. **The backfire timeline.** Test the bet in public. Did the wave of large
   companies replacing people with AI actually reverse? Find the documented
   reversals, the rehires, the abandoned rollouts, and the studies on how many
   AI projects failed to deliver. Then trace whether that pressure rolled
   downhill. **This is the story spine for the launch content, so every beat
   needs a real citation.** If the reversal did not happen, say so. It changes
   the story, and research is how the owner finds out before a camera does.
3. **The competitor picture.** Who sells AI cleanup, AI help, or a part-time
   AI role to small and medium businesses today. Offer, price, positioning,
   delivery, and the gaps. **Look hard for a real competitor layer.**
   Confirming the hole without searching for the thing that would fill it is
   the one way this researcher fails.
4. **Sizing and spend proof.** Two jobs, both required. First, evidence they
   spend: what a business this size pays monthly for AI, what they bought, how
   many abandoned it. Second, COUNT THEM. There is no census code for drowning
   in AI, so size it off adoption surveys, abandonment data, failed-project
   rates, and the count of businesses in the segments that surfaced. Return a
   number with the method attached.
5. **The 4 Signs audit.** Score the market against `references/the-4-signs.md`.
   Every sign needs a sourced percentage or average, not an anecdote. Then
   answer the 2 questions that set the price: which sign is most common, and
   which rung of the ladder does the weight of this market sit on? Mark any
   sign that cannot be sourced as UNSOURCED rather than inventing a figure.
6. **The builders.** Market 2. People who built a small AI thing and want to
   sell AI to businesses but have no niche and fear selling. Who they are, how
   many, where they gather, what they charge when they do sell, and where they
   stall. The 35-build corpus in `references/icm-market-map.md` is the
   evidence. Quote it exactly.

## Step 3: synthesize, the part the owner reads

In this order:

1. **Verdict: PRICE-AND-GO / PIVOT / SKIP.** One line, committed. PIVOT means
   the pain is real but the segment, offer, or price is aimed wrong. Say where
   to aim instead.
2. **The market map.** The 2 or 3 concrete segments actually drowning, ranked,
   with why each earned its rank. This market is horizontal, so a report that
   treats it as one blob has not done the job. Everything after this section
   runs on the top segment, and says so.
3. **The lead pool.** How many of the top segment exist and how that was
   counted. Flag it loudly under about 5,000. A thin pool caps the model no
   matter how real the pain is.
4. **Where they sit on the ladder**, using the rungs in the 4 Signs file. This
   is the pricing model. Name the rung most of the segment sits on.
5. **What it costs them today.** Run the leak model. Human leak and spend leak
   separately, then the total, in hours and dollars per month. Label every
   modeled input. The human leak is usually the bigger number and nobody is
   quoting it to them, so it leads.
6. **The price anchor.** What it costs to hire the person they would otherwise
   hire, loaded. The offer gets compared to that, never to a software
   subscription.
7. **The affordability call.** AFFORDABLE, STRETCH, or UNAFFORDABLE, then the
   arithmetic, judged against the median business in the segment. Close with
   one line: lead with the build, or lead with the monthly.
8. **The 65% floor.** Their side passed, this is the owner's. Use the delivery
   economics file: the stack shipped, expected build and support hours with
   sources, the 3 scenarios, the build floor and retainer floor as 2 plain
   numbers, the carve-outs, and the owned-against-rented table with no
   conclusion written under it. Check the retainer before the build. The
   monthly is where this offer breaks.
9. **Break-even in their units.** Never a percentage. Hours given back per
   month, and the fraction of the salary they were going to pay anyway.
10. **The wedge.** Where nobody else is. It should match the most common sign.
    If it does not, explain why.
11. **The USP.** One sentence, plain words, tied to a real gap.
12. **2 to 3 offer angles** in the segment's verbatim language. Each: promise,
    mechanism, price anchor, which sign it fixes, and it respects the
    affordability call.
13. **3 hooks** built on the formula below.
14. **The pain language bank.** Best verbatim quotes, grouped by theme, with
    sources, plus a words-they-use bank. Using their word is half of sounding
    like an insider.
15. **Content seeds.** 5 to 8 specific things the owner can make from this
    research for the launch lead-up. This research feeds the production line,
    so this section is not a bonus.
16. **What would kill this.** The strongest evidence AGAINST the verdict,
    honestly. Say plainly if the signs had to be modeled rather than sourced.

## The hook formula

7 beats, in order. Every number comes from the research or from what the
prospect already said.

1. **The mess anchor.** You've got 6 or 7 AI things running and you use 2.
2. **The hours overlay.** That's somebody's Tuesday, every week.
3. **The wage.** At what you pay them, that's around $900 a month.
4. **The spend.** On top of the subscriptions you're already carrying.
5. **Their own admission.** The slot. Fill it with what they said on the call.
6. **The alternative.** The other way to fix it is hiring somebody at $70,000.
7. **The ask against that.** This is a fraction of that, once, and it's done.

Rules for the hook: ranges on anything they can dispute. Never say "sign 1" or
"the 4 Signs" out loud to a buyer, say the plain sentence. Beat 5 stays a slot
for live calls. Hours and dollars, never percentages. Never quote a figure
researchers 4 or 5 could not source.

## Step 5: hand off the next move

End both the page and the chat reply with copy-pasteable commands, gated on the
verdict. On PRICE-AND-GO or PIVOT:

```
Attack it:
  "use rymac-build-a-profit-calculator with icm-research-<slug>.md"
  "use rymac-build-a-sales-page for <segment>, research is in icm-research-<slug>.md"
  "use rymac-write-a-headline from icm-research-<slug>.md"
```

On SKIP, print the one thing that would change the answer and none of the
above.

Say in one line that every downstream skill reads the HANDOFF CONTRACT at the
bottom of that file, so nothing asks the owner to re-explain the segment, the
pain, the numbers, or the price.

## Rules

1. **A verdict is required.** "It depends" is not a deliverable.
2. **Name the segments.** A horizontal answer is a failed answer.
3. **Verbatim over paraphrase.** The copy is built from this research.
4. **Every sign maps to a complaint and every complaint maps to a sign.**
5. **The ladder sets the price, not company size.**
6. **Complaining is not demand.** Anchor the verdict in spend evidence.
7. **Never state a spend, wage, or hour figure without a source or a MODELED
   tag with visible arithmetic.** These numbers get said to people who run
   their own books.
8. **Ranges over single numbers** on anything a prospect could dispute.
9. **Check the retainer floor before the build floor.**
10. **65% is a floor, not a target.** Never green-light an offer that cannot
    project it.
11. **Never invent a delivery cost.** Measured or UNMEASURED, and an UNMEASURED
    line widens the low end.
12. **Show owned against rented and write no conclusion under it.** People
    believe what they work out themselves.
13. **If the research breaks the bet, say so plainly and pivot.** That is the
    method working, and it is better content than being right.
14. **Publish the page every single run, unasked.**
15. **Plain English everywhere in the output.** No pipeline, no schema, no
    architecture talk. It is files and folders. Numbers as digits. No em
    dashes. If a salesperson would not say the word on a live call, it does
    not ship.
