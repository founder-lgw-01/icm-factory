# What it costs the owner to deliver one folder system

The mirror of the affordability question. One section of the report asks
whether they can pay. This file is what lets the next section answer whether
The owner makes money.

**The standing rule: never build a thing that cannot project a 65% net margin.**
A floor, not a target. If the projection cannot clear it, the price is wrong,
the offer is wrong, or the hours are wrong. Say which.

## Why this offer costs hours, not minutes

The measured cost card in `~/.claude/skills/rymac-research-a-trade-niche/references/delivery-costs.md`
is built for an AI phone assistant, where the cost is minutes of talking. Those
figures still apply if a phone assistant is ever part of this offer, and the
carve-out rules there hold everywhere.

**A folder system system is not that.** It is a build. Somebody sits down,
learns the business, lays out the filing cabinet, writes the front page, tests
it on real work, and hands it over. The cost is hours.

That means the whole margin question reduces to 2 numbers:

1. How many hours does a build take?
2. What is an hour worth?

Everything else is rounding.

---

## The build side

```
build cost   = build hours x hourly cost
build margin = (price - build cost) / price
```

**Hourly cost is what it costs to get the hour done, not what the owner is worth.**
If the owner does it themselves, use what he would pay a capable person to do it,
because his own hour has an opportunity cost that a margin figure cannot see.
State the rate used, every time.

**What 65% actually demands,** so the constraint is visible before anyone
argues about price:

| Price | Delivery cost must stay under | Hours at $100 | Hours at $150 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1,000 | $350 | 3.5 | 2.3 |
| $2,500 | $875 | 8.75 | 5.8 |
| $3,500 | $1,225 | 12.25 | 8.2 |
| $5,000 | $1,750 | 17.5 | 11.7 |

Read that table before quoting any price. It says a $1,000 build has to be
done in about 3 hours, and it says the top of the owner's band buys real room.

## The retainer side, which is the tighter one

The monthly is where this offer breaks, and it breaks quietly. Check it first.

```
monthly cost   = support hours a month x hourly cost + any hosting the owner carries
monthly margin = (retainer - monthly cost) / retainer
```

**Run the arithmetic and look at it honestly.** At $150 an hour, a $500 monthly
is fully consumed by 3.3 hours of work. At 2 hours a month the margin is about
40%, which is under the floor. To clear 65% on $500, the monthly support has to
fit inside roughly 1.1 hours.

| Retainer | Support hours allowed at $150 | At $100 |
|---|---|---|
| $300 | 0.7 | 1.05 |
| $500 | 1.2 | 1.75 |
| $700 | 1.6 | 2.45 |

**So the report must say what the monthly actually buys.** A retainer that
promises open-ended help fails the floor at every price in the owner's band. A
retainer scoped to a set check-in plus a capped number of changes can clear it.
Naming that scope is part of the deliverable, not a detail for later.

If a segment needs more hand-holding than the scope allows, that is a finding.
Raise the retainer, cap the scope, or skip the segment.

## The 3 scenarios

Always 3, never 1. State the assumptions on each.

- **Low end.** A messy business, the build runs long, support runs heavy, the
  higher hourly rate.
- **Mid.** Expected hours for the segment, at the expected rate. This is the
  number that decides.
- **High end.** A clean business, a repeatable build, light support, the lower
  rate.

Expected hours come from the research, not from a wish. A business with 4
locations and 33 properties is not the same build as a single shop, and the
report has to say which one it priced.

---

## The floor

Solve for it and state it plainly, both halves:

1. **The build floor.** The lowest up-front price that still clears 65% at
   expected hours.
2. **The retainer floor.** The lowest monthly that still clears 65% at the
   expected support hours and the scope named above.

One number each. The owner does not have to sell there. He wants to know where
the ground is.

**The veteran floor, separately.** The owner may knowingly run thinner for
veteran-owned businesses because the wins come from everywhere else. Give that
second pair of numbers and label it a deliberate choice, never the business
model.

**If the floor lands above what the segment can afford, the offer is broken.**
Say so loudly and name the fix: raise the price, shrink the build, cap the
retainer scope, or skip the segment. Those 2 sections disagreeing is a finding,
not a rounding error.

## Carve-outs

Never inside the margin math. Name each one so nobody adds it back later.

- **Their own AI subscriptions and usage.** They keep their own accounts and
  pay their own bills. They own it, which is the entire point of the offer.
- **Their own domain, hosting, and software seats.**
- **Text messaging, if it ever appears in the stack.** The client registers
  their own sending brand and buys their own number, so they own the number and
  the owner does not own the carrier liability.

Anything billed directly to the client gets the same treatment: named, and
excluded from cost.

## Owned against rented

Put the same delivered outcome on both sets of rails, side by side.

| | What they run now | folder system |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly software | the stack of subscriptions the research found | their own accounts, kept |
| Who operates it | a person, part time, badly | the filing cabinet |
| Who owns the files | whoever they signed up with | them |

**Fill both columns from the research and stop.** Write no conclusion under it,
do not name your product, do not explain why owning is better. The arithmetic makes
the argument, and a reader who gets there himself believes it. Same law as the
calculator: never tell them anything they can be brought to say themselves.

---

## What is not measured yet

**Build hours are UNMEASURED.** Nobody has timed a real folder system
build start to finish. Every hour figure in a report is MODELED until he does,
and it gets labeled that way, with the arithmetic shown.

Until then:

1. Widen the low end rather than inventing a tighter estimate.
2. Say in the report that the floor rests on a modeled hour count.
3. The first real build gets timed, and this file gets the actual number.

A margin projection resting on a guessed cost is worse than admitting there is
no projection yet. The owner quotes these numbers to people who run their own books.
